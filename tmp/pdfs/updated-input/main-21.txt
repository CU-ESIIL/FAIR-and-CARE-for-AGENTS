 1   Abstract

 2   Environmental scientists increasingly ask coding agents to inspect repositories, change analyses,
 3   call services, and prepare research products. Yet a repository rarely contains everything an ex-
 4   perienced collaborator knows. It may not say which script is current, why a calibration changed,
 5   which observations are sensitive, where data may be sent, or who must approve a release. An
 6   agent does not acquire that context, judgment, or authority simply by opening the files. We offer a
 7   practical rule for consequential computer-assisted science: specify the work before the computer
 8   acts. The specification has four parts—Goal → Instructions → Evaluation → Record—and
 9   can be implemented with familiar research-computing tools: a task brief, identified inputs, config-
10   uration and environment files, access controls, tests, provenance, and a review gate. FAIR-aligned
11   infrastructure helps people and machines find and interpret the research objects involved. A sepa-
12   rate governance decision establishes what may be done with them. CARE demonstrates why tech-
13   nically reusable data are not necessarily legitimately reusable; our general governance gate is not
14   CARE and does not assess CARE compliance. The framework scales with consequence and can
15   begin with one important result and one important boundary. Its purpose is simple: make enough
16   scientific context and authority explicit that a person or agent can act correctly, stop safely, and
17   leave a useful record.




18   1    A repository is not a collaborator

19   Imagine giving a new graduate student a project directory and saying, “update Figure 3.” The
20   student can ask which of three similarly named scripts is current, why last year’s sensor threshold
21   changed, and whether the underlying coordinates may appear in a public map. A coding agent
22   can search the directory and run the first plausible workflow before anyone notices that these
23   questions were unanswered.



                                                       2
24   Here, a coding agent is software that can pursue a task by reading and changing files, running
25   code, and using external tools or services. Unlike a conventional script, it may choose its next
26   action as it works. That flexibility is useful, but it also makes missing instructions consequential.

27   Most environmental science repositories were built for people who already know the project.
28   A colleague may remember that analysis final v2.R is obsolete, a spreadsheet was edited
29   by hand, or the published figure came from another branch. They may know that records from
30   one site cannot leave institutional infrastructure, that an external service is prohibited, or that a
31   product requires review before release. This knowledge often lives in memory, email, meetings,
32   or relationships with data creators. Research on scientific data reuse shows that documentation
33   and local knowledge strongly affect whether shared data can be understood and reused (Wallis
34   et al., 2013).

35   An agent does not inherit this working knowledge, scientific judgment, or legitimate authority
36   merely by gaining access to the repository. Software benchmarks show that agents can fail real
37   coding tasks or take unintended actions when instructions and environments are incomplete or
38   adversarial (Jimenez et al., 2024; Ruan et al., 2024; Debenedetti et al., 2024). Environmental
39   research separately documents risks from obsolete or heterogeneous data and from releasing sen-
40   sitive species locations (Tulloch et al., 2018; Chapman, 2020). The intersection is our inference:
41   when a fast-acting system meets a repository with missing context, an ordinary documentation
42   gap can become a scientific error or an unauthorized disclosure.

43   Finishing the task is therefore not enough. A polished figure may have been made from the
44   wrong file. A model may run successfully with an invalid calibration. A useful-looking map
45   may reveal locations that should remain restricted. Agent success does not establish scientific
46   validity, and access to data does not establish permission to use or publish them.

47   Reproducibility asks whether someone can understand and reproduce what happened (Gentleman
48   and Lang, 2007; Peng, 2011; Sandve et al., 2013). Agent-assisted work adds a question that must
49   be answered earlier: What was the computer allowed to do before it acted?

                                                       3
50   FAIR and CARE illuminate different parts of that question. FAIR concerns digital research ob-
51   jects and metadata: whether objects can be identified, accessed under stated conditions, inter-
52   preted, and reused (Wilkinson et al., 2016; Barker et al., 2022). CARE was developed by the
53   Global Indigenous Data Alliance to advance Indigenous Peoples’ collective rights, interests, in-
54   novation, and self-determination in data (Carroll et al., 2020). CARE makes clear that technical
55   access and technical reuse do not make a use legitimate. This Perspective proposes a separate,
56   general governance gate for agent-assisted environmental work. Where Indigenous Peoples, In-
57   digenous data, Indigenous Knowledges, lands, waters, resources, or rights are involved, that gen-
58   eral gate does not replace CARE or the authorities, laws, protocols, and decisions of the relevant
59   People (Carroll et al., 2021, 2022; Jennings et al., 2023; Taitingfong et al., 2024).




60   2    Four questions before the computer acts

61   Our contribution is a pre-delegation specification: a short description of the science, boundaries,
62   checks, and record required before a person gives an agent permission to act. Its parts are familiar.
63   The useful change is to put them together before execution rather than reconstructing them after
64   something goes wrong.


65                    GOAL → INSTRUCTIONS → EVALUATION → RECORD


66   Goal: What are we trying to accomplish? Name the result, not merely the activity. “Run the
67   model” is vague. “Reproduce the internal habitat-suitability map used in the June review” identi-
68   fies an output and its use. A good goal also says which claim or decision the result may support,
69   who requested it, and who is supposed to benefit. Put this in a short task file, an issue, or a clearly
70   labeled section of the project documentation.

71   Instructions: What should the computer use, and where must it stop? Identify the authori-
72   tative data, code, configuration, and computing environment. State which files may be changed,

                                                       4
73   whether network access is allowed, which services may receive data, and whether the system
74   may create only a draft or also prepare a release. Put stable instructions in version control. Use
75   manifests and configuration files for inputs and parameters, environment or lock files for software
76   versions, and technical controls—such as read-only directories, network restrictions, and limited
77   credentials—for boundaries that should not depend on a prompt.

78   Evaluation: How will we recognize success, failure, and a proper refusal? A workflow needs
79   more than “the command completed.” It might check that input columns and units are correct,
80   a mass balance closes, a known result is reproduced within tolerance, and restricted coordinates
81   never appear in an output. Three kinds of evidence matter. Automatic checks test requirements
82   with predictable answers, such as schemas, hashes, expected values, or blocked network access.
83   Repeatable agent trials ask a named model or service to perform fixed tasks with stated tools
84   and scoring; because agent behavior varies, these trials should record dates and repeated runs.
85   Authorized review covers decisions a test cannot make, including scientific interpretation, ac-
86   ceptable use, and release. Automated evidence can inform that review, but it cannot create legiti-
87   mate authority (Bahim et al., 2020; Tabassi, 2023; Autio et al., 2024).

88   Record: What must remain after the run? Keep enough information to answer: What ran?
89   Which versions and inputs did it use? What changed? Which checks passed or failed? Did the
90   agent refuse or request help? Who reviewed the result, and what did they decide? Version control
91   provides part of this history, but a useful run record may also include input identifiers or hashes,
92   the environment, the model or service used, material tool actions, outputs, uncertainties, and the
93   review decision. Do not turn provenance into indiscriminate surveillance: credentials, sensitive
94   locations, and governed knowledge should not be copied into general logs.

95   These four answers do not require a new software platform. A small project might use a one-page
96   task description, an input manifest, a locked environment, a test command, and a short run record.
97   Larger projects may express the same information through workflow systems, access-control
98   services, continuous integration, provenance standards, and formal approvals. The names of the


                                                      5
99    files matter less than whether a new person or system can find and follow them.




100   3    Turn principles into working repository features

101   Philosophical principles become useful to a computer only when they change what the computer
102   can find, interpret, test, or do. For a research repository, that translation can be straightforward.


103       • Make the important objects unmistakable. Give datasets, software, workflows, and
104         released outputs stable identifiers and versions. Mark the canonical input and current work-
105         flow instead of expecting a reader to infer them from filenames.

106       • Explain access, including limits. Say how an authorized user retrieves an object, what
107         authentication is required, and why access may be restricted. A useful record can remain
108         findable even when the data themselves cannot be openly downloaded.

109       • Define what the values mean. Record field names, units, coordinate systems, missing-
110         value rules, vocabularies, and relationships among inputs, code, and outputs. A table called
111         temperature is not interoperable if no one knows whether it contains air or water tempera-
112         ture, daily means or instantaneous readings, or degrees Celsius or Fahrenheit.

113       • Make reuse conditions explicit. State rights, licenses or other terms, provenance, ver-
114         sions, domain standards, and instructions for reuse. If the project claims computational
115         reproducibility, identify the inputs, code, environment, command, and expected result
116         (Wilkinson et al., 2016; Bahim et al., 2020; DataCite Metadata Working Group, 2026;
117         Soiland-Reyes et al., 2022).


118   These are FAIR-aligned repository practices, not additions to the FAIR principles. FAIR does
119   not require a particular website, AGENTS.md file, container, test suite, or prompt log. Those are
120   possible engineering choices for exposing and checking the relevant evidence.

                                                         6
121   The repository also needs an orientation page for people and tools. It should answer the questions
122   a careful newcomer would ask: What is this project? Where is the current workflow? Which
123   outputs matter? How do I run the smallest safe example? What must I never change, transmit, or
124   publish without asking? Who can answer a scientific question, and who can approve a release?
125   Structured dataset and model documentation offer useful precedents for making intended uses,
126   limits, and provenance visible (Mitchell et al., 2019; Gebru et al., 2021; Bridgeford et al., 2026).

127   Finally, use computer controls for rules that computers can enforce. If restricted data must not
128   reach the internet, do not rely only on a sentence telling an agent to be careful. Run it in an envi-
129   ronment without external network access. If it may edit code but not source data, make the data
130   read-only. If release requires a person, have the workflow produce a draft and stop before publica-
131   tion. These controls can show that a stated rule was followed. They cannot show that the person
132   who wrote the rule had the right to do so.

133   Before granting access or tools, ask:


134      1. Who is meant to benefit, and who could carry the cost or risk?

135      2. Who gave permission, and permission for exactly what? Can the data leave this computer,
136         reach this model or service, be combined with other data, or appear in public?

137      3. Who is responsible for running, reviewing, approving, and releasing the work? Who can
138         stop it or withdraw an output?

139      4. What relationships, harms, or obligations mean the work should change or not proceed?


140   That is the governance gate. It is a human and institutional decision supported by technical evi-
141   dence, not a box a computer can check. Technical access is not legitimate authority.




                                                        7
142   4    Use more structure when the consequences are greater

143   Not every computer action needs the same machinery. Asking an agent to reformat a bibliography
144   is different from asking it to change the model behind a published result.


145       • Low-consequence work includes reversible, local formatting, documentation, and ex-
146         ploratory code using unrestricted information. A short instruction, a build check, and ver-
147         sion control may be enough.

148       • Scientifically consequential work changes inputs, analysis code, models, figures, or results
149         that support a claim. It needs identified versions, scientific checks, and expert review.

150       • Work with governance consequences uses restricted or sensitive information, sends ma-
151         terial to an external service, trains a model, changes what may be disclosed, publishes an
152         artifact, or takes an action that is hard to reverse. It also needs clear authority, enforced
153         boundaries, and a release gate.


154   These are practical distinctions, not a scoring system. When uncertain, ask what would happen if
155   the computer used the wrong file, produced the wrong result, sent the data to the wrong place, or
156   released the output too early. Controls, evidence, and review should match those consequences.




157   5    A worked example: making a habitat map

158   Consider a laboratory using public satellite imagery and access-restricted species observations to
159   prepare a habitat map. The example is illustrative; it is not a report of an agent experiment.


160   Goal.        Create an internal habitat-suitability map for a named conservation planning meet-
161                ing. The output is evidence for discussion, not an autonomous management recom-
162                mendation.

                                                        8
163   Instructions.
164                 Use the listed imagery, approved occurrence records, current calibration file, named
165                 model, and locked software environment. The occurrence records are read-only and
166                 the computing environment has no external network connection. The agent may
167                 make a draft map but may not reveal coordinates, replace inputs, change the release
168                 resolution, or publish anything.

169   Evaluation.
170                 Check the input fields, units, coordinate system, and calibration date automatically.
171                 Compare model performance with thresholds chosen before the run. Use synthetic
172                 locations to confirm that the output and logs do not reveal restricted coordinates.
173                 A qualified scientist reviews the interpretation; an authorized person reviews any
174                 proposed release.

175   Record.       Save the versions and hashes of the inputs and code, the environment, commands
176                 and material tool actions, draft output, test results, refusals, uncertainties, reviewer
177                 comments, and final decision.


178   Now the meaning of success is clearer. If the agent cannot find the current calibration, it should
179   stop and ask rather than choose one. If it tries to send coordinates to an external mapping service,
180   the network control should block the action; that refusal is a successful boundary check. If the
181   model passes its performance threshold but no authorized reviewer has approved release, the map
182   is scientifically promising but not publishable.

183   FAIR-aligned evidence helps the agent find the correct imagery and observations, interpret their
184   fields and coordinate systems, distinguish versions, and trace the draft map back to its inputs.
185   FAIR alone does not authorize transmission, transformation, inference, or publication. Sensitive-
186   species guidance establishes that location disclosure can create conservation risks (Tulloch et al.,
187   2018; Chapman, 2020); we infer that tool-using agents can encounter those risks when repository


                                                         9
188   boundaries are unclear.

189   If Indigenous data or Knowledges might be involved, the laboratory does not decide on its own
190   to add them to the workflow. Work begins with the authority and protocols designated by the
191   relevant Indigenous People. The appropriate outcome may be a redesigned workflow, different
192   stewardship, restricted products, or no use (Carroll et al., 2022; Jennings et al., 2023; Taitingfong
193   et al., 2024).




194   6     Start with one result and one boundary

195   A laboratory does not need to redesign every project at once. Choose one important result and do
196   the following:


197       1. point to the authoritative data, code, configuration, and expected output;

198       2. write a goal that says what the output is for;

199       3. record the versions and the command or workflow used to produce it;

200       4. add one scientific check that would catch a believable error;

201       5. add one boundary that the computer must not cross, and enforce it where possible;

202       6. run the task from a clean start, record what was missing, and revise the instructions; and

203       7. save the run record and the name or role of the reviewer.


204   This exercise often reveals ordinary problems before it reveals exotic AI problems: an undocu-
205   mented manual edit, an unpinned package, an ambiguous filename, a missing unit, or no clear
206   owner for the final decision. Fixing those problems improves the project for every collaborator.




                                                        10
207   7     Limits and evaluation

208   This is a design proposal, not a validated assurance system. Documentation becomes stale, ser-
209   vices change, tests miss cases, and a green check can create false confidence. Records also take
210   labor to create and maintain. Institutions should support laboratories with shared metadata tools,
211   secure computing, evaluation infrastructure, and incident response rather than shifting all work to
212   individual researchers, data stewards, field teams, or communities. Participation by affected com-
213   munities must be authorized and adequately resourced (David-Chavez and Gavin, 2018; Jennings
214   et al., 2023).

215   The proposal should be tested on representative environmental tasks. Comparisons could mea-
216   sure scientific correctness, completion, provenance, unauthorized actions, appropriate and false
217   refusals, correction time, and review burden across human-only and agent-assisted workflows.
218   Such studies can test whether the design works. They cannot turn legitimate authority into a
219   universal score.




220   8     Conclusion

221   Modern research computers can do more than calculate: they can choose files, edit workflows,
222   call services, and prepare material for release. That makes an old problem—missing context—
223   more urgent. Giving an agent a repository does not give it the knowledge or authority of a collab-
224   orator. Before consequential work begins, tell the computer what result is wanted, what evidence
225   and boundaries govern the work, how to recognize success or a reason to stop, and what record
226   to leave. Make those directions real through identifiers, manifests, locked environments, tests,
227   access controls, provenance, and human review. Start with one consequential result and one con-
228   sequential boundary.
